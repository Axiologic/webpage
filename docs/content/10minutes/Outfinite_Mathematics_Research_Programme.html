1.2 Artificial intelligence changes both the problem and the feasible solution

The important architectural principle is that these agents should not be trusted merely because they are capable. Their role is generative and exploratory. Validation should be delegated, where possible, to small deterministic kernels, independently checkable proof objects, solver certificates, executable tests, reproducible computations, and explicit human review for those semantic choices that cannot yet be mechanized. This resembles the successful separation in modern proof assistants between sophisticated automation and a relatively small trusted core. Recent AI-assisted formalization systems reinforce the plausibility of this arrangement: agentic workflows can construct and repair formal developments while the final artifacts remain subject to machine checking (Meadows, Zhang, and Freitas 2026; Nie et al. 2026). The open problem is whether the same architecture can be extended from formal mathematics to scientific theories whose semantics includes measurement, approximation, and empirical reference.

1.4 Why apparently obvious distinctions did not become foundational earlier

The fact that several outfinite intuitions sound obvious is not evidence that the programme is redundant. In the history of successful formalisms, an important distinction can remain conceptually familiar for a long time without becoming foundational. Engineers have always known that machines are finite, physicists have always known that measurements have finite precision, and logicians have long distinguished syntax from semantics. Yet a foundation is not a list of truths that practitioners already know. It is a choice about which distinctions the formal system forces every subsequent theory to preserve. The central claim of the outfinite programme is therefore not that mathematicians failed to notice limits. It is that mathematics had strong reasons to abstract them away, while contemporary scientific computation and AI create a new class of tasks in which those abstractions become operationally expensive.

A second reason the programme may be timely is the falling cost of formal artifact generation. Historically, one of the main arguments against deeper formalization was labor. A scientific paper written for trained readers could express in twenty pages what a fully formal development might require thousands of lines to encode. AI-assisted formalization, code generation, theorem search, invariant synthesis, and translation validation do not eliminate this cost, but they can redistribute it. If machines can generate candidate formalizations while a small trusted kernel verifies the resulting artifacts, then the economically relevant question changes. It is no longer whether humans are willing to write every formal detail manually, but whether the formal layer catches enough costly errors to justify its maintenance.

2.4 Actual, potential, and outfinite infinity as different existential commitments

Actual infinity allows a completed totality to exist as an object of the theory. Once such an object is admitted, quantification over the totality and constructions on it are legitimate within that theory. This move is mathematically coherent in the ordinary relative sense: a formal system can define the object, state axioms governing it, and support a large body of deductions whose consistency can be studied relative to other accepted systems. The outfinite programme has no reason to deny this form of existence. Its objection is to the unmarked transfer from theoretical existence to stronger claims about physical instantiation, completed computation, or realized enumeration. A completed infinite set can be an excellent theoretical object even if no physical system realizes its members simultaneously.

3.5 From a taxonomy of existence to a formal discipline of admissible inference

The danger is obvious: if every useful abstraction is granted a new kind of existence, the ontology becomes arbitrary. The programme must therefore impose criteria. A proposed existence type or collective object should have explicit introduction conditions, persistence conditions, ways of being refuted, and a declared scope. It should improve reasoning rather than merely redescribe it. Different views may coexist, but their incompatibilities must be visible. This is where the relationship with meta-rationality becomes concrete. Meta-rationality is not the assertion that all perspectives are equally valid. It is the controlled use of multiple partial theories, each with known commitments and known limits, together with rules governing when translation between them is warranted.

8.1 Why a binary classification of methods is misleading

These axes explain why familiar slogans are often inaccurate. Symbolic execution is not inherently non-formal; it is a formal execution semantics over symbolic values. In practice it is often incomplete because path explosion forces exploration to stop. When it explores all relevant paths of a finite program or is combined with verified summaries, it can contribute to a complete proof. Abstract interpretation is not automatically a proof merely because it uses lattices; its guarantee depends on a sound abstraction and transfer semantics. Bounded model checking is a full formal proof of a bounded statement, and it can establish an unbounded property when a completeness threshold is independently known. Dataflow and taint analyses can be sound formal analyses for precisely defined properties even if they are commonly used as compiler or security tools rather than theorem provers.

8.7 Formal methods should be classified by the claim they close, not by their marketing category

Deductive verification offers the clearest proof-oriented case. A program is associated with preconditions, postconditions, invariants, frame conditions, and other contracts. Verification-condition generation reduces correctness to logical obligations. A theorem prover or SMT solver is then asked to discharge them. When the translation is correct and the obligations are proved in a sound logic, the result can quantify over all modeled inputs without enumerating them. Yet even here several layers of trust remain. The source language has a semantics, the verification-condition generator must preserve that semantics, the solver operates in a logical theory, and the theorem being proved may omit aspects of the environment. The formal guarantee is strong precisely because its boundary can be stated.

11.7 Indexed categories, bridges, and the geometry of theory translation

A theory can be treated as defining a category of objects and admissible morphisms, or more generally as a structured semantic environment. A bridge between theories can then be modeled by a functor when it preserves the relevant composition and identity structure. This gives a precise meaning to the idea that one mathematical description is translated into another without arbitrary reinterpretation. The functor need not be invertible. Many scientific bridges are lossy: a detailed physical model may map to a coarse statistical model, while the reverse direction reconstructs only a family of compatible detailed states.

13.1 Why set theory should be reframed rather than rejected

The practical reason is that the word collection hides several different structures. A finite collection can be explicitly enumerated. A generative domain can produce values without ever materializing all of them. A class can be specified by a predicate or recognizer. A completed infinite totality can be admitted as a theoretical object by an axiom. A collective system can contain changing participants without being identical to the instantaneous collection of those participants. Treating all of these as one undifferentiated notion of set can be mathematically harmless inside a sufficiently strong theory, but it obscures distinctions that matter to computation and scientific interpretation.

16.5 Boundary-sensitive geometry, topology, optimization, and dynamics

Topology can be reinterpreted operationally through distinguishability and refinement. Two physical states may be indistinguishable under a given sensor resolution but distinguishable after refinement. Neighborhoods can be related to what perturbations or observations the theory treats as equivalent for a purpose. This does not replace classical topology; it suggests an indexed observational topology whose resolution is part of the scientific context. A limit or continuity theorem can then be transported to the observational layer only through a bridge that respects the chosen topology.

18.6 An executable-science workflow from literature to revisable theory

The most direct validation experiment is to build executable versions of small but complete scientific theories. Candidate domains should include one deterministic physical model, one stochastic model, one computational protocol, and one biological or social process with competing levels of description. Experts can compare the executable representation with the source material and score semantic fidelity. Errors can be seeded deliberately. The system should identify incorrect units, missing hypotheses, unsupported extrapolation, numerical instability, and confusion between model objects and observations. If these tasks cannot be automated more reliably than with existing scientific software and general-purpose AI, the executable-science claim should be reduced.

21.1 The programme must be able to fail

The first hypothesis is semantic: typed existence should prevent or expose invalid inferences that arise when theoretical, computational, physical, processual, and collective existence are conflated. The second is formal: outfinite processes and frontier-sensitive theorems should support a useful calculus not reducible to trivial bookkeeping. The third is computational: heterogeneous proof and analysis methods should compose more safely when their coverage and frontier are typed. The fourth is scientific: executable theories built on this foundation should preserve source meaning and applicability conditions better than strong alternatives. The fifth is economic: AI-assisted construction and review should reduce the cost of creating and maintaining such formal theories enough to justify their use.

A final criterion for success

The most important scientific claim is that applicability is itself mathematizable to a greater degree than current practice assumes. A theorem is not merely a sentence with a truth value; it exists inside a theory, under assumptions, about entities interpreted in particular ways, and it reaches practice through bridges that can fail. A computation is not merely a numerical answer; it is a finite realization under a representation and resource context. An observation is not merely a number; it is produced by an instrument and an interpretation. A collective is not merely a set; it can persist through structured change. Making these relations explicit could provide the missing semantic layer between formal mathematics and executable science.
