The Technology Used Before It Becomes Legitimate

Generative artificial intelligence entered precisely this ambiguous zone. Within three years, some aggregate estimates indicated a diffusion speed unprecedented for a consumer technology, while official business statistics still showed operational integration to be a minority practice. The Stanford AI Index 2026 estimates that generative tools reached approximately 53 percent population adoption in three years. The OECD reports that in 2025 more than one third of individuals in member countries had used such tools, while only 20.2 percent of firms reported using artificial intelligence; the gap between large and small firms was enormous, 52 percent versus 17.4 percent (Stanford HAI, 2026; OECD, 2026a). These figures are not contradictory. They measure different things and reveal the very subject of this book: trying a technology can become almost ordinary long before dependence on it, workflow integration, and institutional recognition become normal.

This book proposes eight concepts that organize the phenomenon. The Abnormal User Principle explains why initial users are selected from unusual situations and do not represent the future market. The Slop Paradox explains why the frontier of quality can rise while the average quality of visible artifacts falls. The Visibility Inversion shows why AI failures are labelled and public while competent uses dissolve into the final result or remain hidden. The Permission Threshold separates technical utility from social legitimacy. The Permission Cascade explains the moment when each new user reduces perceived risk for the next. The Expertise Paradox distinguishes between those who receive the largest immediate gain and those able to extract the greatest strategic value. Apprenticeship Debt names the accumulated cost when organizations automate the work through which future experts used to become experts. The Inevitability Radar turns these observations into an analytical instrument for entrepreneurs, investors, and marketers.

Adoption Is Not an Opinion

For an entrepreneur, the difference between use and transformation is essential. The first level can produce rapid user growth and temporary revenue. The second creates recurring budgets, integration, proprietary data, and switching costs. A product can be viral without becoming infrastructure; it can be infrastructure in a niche without being viral; or it can move from one to the other when individual behavior forces the organization to recognize a practice that is already widespread. Microsoft documented an early form of this transition in 2024: 78 percent of workplace AI users brought their own tools, and the behavior crossed generations. At the same time, 52 percent hesitated to disclose using AI for important tasks, while 53 percent feared that doing so would make them appear replaceable (Microsoft, 2024). The market existed before the institution granted it permission.

Higher rungs require a different product and a different kind of evidence. The user must know when an output is good enough, who is accountable for an error, how data enter and where they go, how the system can be corrected, what happens when the model changes, and whether the advantage persists once competitors gain access to the same capability. Where verification costs exceed the time saved, “adoption” may merely move effort from the creator to the recipient. The phenomenon called workslop describes apparently finished content that transfers to colleagues the work of discovering its lack of substance. Existing commercial evidence remains too early for broad conclusions, but the strategic signal is clear: generated volume is not equivalent to created value.

The Slop Paradox

Slop is not defined only by low quality. It is also a problem in the distribution of effort. A document generated quickly may look good enough to send, yet force the recipient to identify its assumptions, repeat verification, and discover whether a real idea exists behind the language. The author saves time by transferring cost to the reader. In organizations, this externalization can turn local productivity into systemic unproductivity. Commercial research by BetterUp Labs and the Stanford Social Media Lab popularized the term workslop for such outputs; because it was a survey study rather than an independent measurement of productivity, its figures should be treated cautiously. The concept remains useful: technology can move work rather than eliminate it.

Criticism also has a limit. A decline in the average quality of public outputs does not mean the frontier lacks economic importance. Markets do not always pay for the average. A studio can use AI to explore thousands of alternatives and publish one. A researcher can generate ten hypotheses and retain only what survives verification. A law firm can use a model for search and structure without delivering the raw answer to a client. In such cases, abundance remains internal and selection protects the exterior. Slop becomes dangerous when the filter disappears, not when alternatives appear.

The Personality of Adoption

The practical interpretations should remain probabilistic. Openness may increase experimentation, but experimentation is not retention. Conscientiousness may favor systems that provide structure, reliability, and visible progress, yet the same trait may produce rejection when outputs are difficult to verify. Extraversion may support social learning and peer-led diffusion, although it is not required for sustained individual use. Higher neuroticism or anxiety may increase avoidance of uncertain systems, but anxiety can also produce reliance when AI is marketed as reassurance. Agreeableness may facilitate cooperative interaction with an assistant in one context and preference for human help in another. The same trait can support opposite behaviors when product design changes the perceived risk.

The Permission Threshold

The permission threshold is the cumulative level of evidence, control, legitimacy, and safety that an individual or institution requires before turning a possibility into normal behavior. It is not identical to intention to use in classical technology-acceptance models, although it builds on them. The Technology Acceptance Model established the importance of perceived usefulness and ease of use, while UTAUT integrated performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003). AI adds a sharper problem: the product can participate in producing judgment, not merely in executing a command. The user must therefore grant permission to delegate part of the competence by which the user is evaluated.

Permission Cascades and Dense Islands

Managers function as nodes with unusual power. They influence not only opinion but also the allocation of time, access, protection, and rewards. Gallup reports that managerial support and workflow integration are associated with much more frequent AI use (Gallup, 2026c). A manager can lower the threshold with a clear statement: this use is permitted, experimentation time is legitimate, and controlled failures will not be punished. A manager can also block the market through ambiguity: ‘innovate, but take no risks; use AI, but own every error completely; save time, but keep every old obligation.’

Productivity Without Learning and the Apprenticeship Debt

A 2025 CHI study of 319 knowledge workers and 936 examples of use found that greater confidence in AI was associated with less self-reported critical effort, while confidence in one’s own ability was associated with more critical thinking. The authors also observed a shift of effort from producing information toward verification, integration, and supervision (Lee et al., 2025). The result does not prove long-term cognitive decline, but it describes a role transition. The question is not whether the person thinks less at every stage; it is whether the person knows how to think in the new stage.

Marketing a Technology That Does Not Yet Have Permission

Traditional marketing assumes that a product offers a known benefit within an intelligible category. AI marketing often has to sell the capability, the new behavior, the right to practice it, and the criterion by which the result should be judged—all at once. The customer must do more than choose a supplier. The customer must accept that part of the work can be reorganized and that this reorganization does not compromise identity, accountability, or position. Marketing adoption is therefore closer to designing an institution than to writing a slogan.

The Graveyard of Apparently Inevitable Ideas

Google Glass combined the capital of a dominant company, fascination with augmented reality, and an easily understood demonstration. Yet the consumer edition was withdrawn from sale in 2015, while Google ended sales of the Enterprise Edition in March 2023 and support in September of the same year (Google, 2023). Reasons included price, battery life, unclear utility, and social discomfort with cameras worn in public space. The capability was real, but the permission threshold was not merely individual. Other people in the room also had to accept the object on the user’s face.

After Consensus, the Technology Disappears

The book began from a provocative hypothesis: part of the aversion to AI may resemble the hesitation of people unwilling to cross the adoption gap first. Research made the hypothesis less comfortable and more valuable. Resistance is sometimes conformity, sometimes status defense, and sometimes a correct assessment of a weak product. Early users may be visionary, desperate, independent, privileged, or simply curious. Senior people may extract superior leverage and adopt more slowly; beginners may gain more in performance and lose more in formation. Personality matters, but competence, task, and environment may matter more.
