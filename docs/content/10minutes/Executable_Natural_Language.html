The Structural Core of English

Modern English is structurally analytic compared with many Indo-European relatives. It relies heavily on word order, auxiliary verbs, prepositions, articles, pronouns, and lexical choice rather than on extensive case or agreement morphology. The basic declarative clause is often described through a subject followed by a verb phrase, yet this familiar formula conceals several layers. The subject is a syntactic position, not a stable semantic role. It may denote an agent, experiencer, patient, theme, instrument, location, event, proposition, dummy element, or formally raised constituent. The verb phrase can contain auxiliaries that mark tense, aspect, modality, voice, and negation, while complements and adjuncts distribute information about participants, circumstances, and discourse status (Huddleston and Pullum 2002).

English transitivity is similarly flexible. Verbs may appear without an object, with one object, with two object-like complements, with a predicative complement, or inside constructions that change the normal alignment between syntax and semantic roles. ‘The glass broke’ and ‘Alice broke the glass’ share a change but differ in explicit causation. ‘Alice gave Bob the file’ and ‘Alice gave the file to Bob’ package the same transfer with different information structure. ‘The committee elected Alice chair’ combines an affected participant with a resultative role. Construction Grammar is useful here because it treats argument-structure patterns as conventional pairings of form and meaning rather than assuming that the lexical verb alone supplies every participant relation (Goldberg 2006).

Relativity, Salience, and the Ethics of Comparison

Number offers both dramatic evidence and a warning against exaggeration. Work with Pirahã and Mundurukú speakers distinguishes approximate numerical competence from exact symbolic number. People without an extensive conventional counting system can estimate and compare quantities, but exact matching becomes difficult when memory, transformation, or displacement prevents direct one-to-one correspondence (Gordon 2004; Pica et al. 2004; Frank et al. 2008). Exact number words appear to function as a cognitive technology: they stabilise cardinal values across time and operations. Yet these studies do not show that a language permanently seals numerical capacity. Training, material practices, and education matter. Recent work also demonstrates that semantic task structure can outweigh language-specific metaphors. A preregistered 2026 study across four languages found that the semantic content of arithmetic problems, not the languages’ preferred temporal metaphors, drove ordinal versus cardinal strategies (John, Macedo-Rouet, and Gros 2026). Relativity is a family of conditional effects, not a master key.

Philosophers use clouds to stage the Problem of the Many. Around the vague object are many overlapping aggregates of droplets, each nearly as good a candidate as the others for being the cloud. The claim that infinity has “no counterpart anywhere in the real world” is too strong. Whether physical space, time, divisibility, field values, or the cosmos instantiate any form of actual infinity is a live question in physics and metaphysics. Some theories contain infinities; some physicists treat singularities as signs of theoretical breakdown; cosmological observations do not settle whether the universe is spatially finite. The responsible claim is narrower: human beings do not encounter a completed infinite collection through ordinary perception, yet formal infinity has transformed mathematics, theology, cosmology, economics, and culture. The Stanford Encyclopedia’s recent survey rightly treats mathematical, physical, cosmological, and theological infinity as related but distinct problems (Easwaran et al. 2025).

Alexandra Aikhenvald and the typology of evidence Aikhenvald’s Evidentiality (2004) is the indispensable survey of grammatical systems that mark information source. It shows that languages may require distinctions such as visual, non-visual sensory, inferred, assumed, or reported evidence, and that these systems vary in both structure and discourse use. The work also separates evidentiality from simple certainty: indirect evidence need not be weak evidence, and direct perception need not be reliable. This distinction is foundational for the proposed epistemic checksum. English commonly compresses source, confidence, trust, and truth into vague expressions such as apparently or I think. Aikhenvald shows that source mode is a legitimate grammatical dimension in its own right.
