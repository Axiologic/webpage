PREFACE

That change also destabilizes the social meaning of elites. Historically, elites did not merely extract. They also organized armies, financed infrastructure, preserved knowledge, administered law, mediated long-distance exchange, and absorbed risks that others could not. These functions never justified every privilege, and they often coexisted with exploitation. Yet a future in which machine systems cheapen expertise and management may expose privileges that were previously defended as payments for scarce competence. The likely result is not an egalitarian vacuum. Humans compete for status even when material goods are plentiful. New elites may arise around compute, energy, network control, cultural legitimacy, security, trust, and the capacity to define institutional goals. The problem becomes how to make elite status functional, contestable, and revocable rather than hereditary in fact even when it is not hereditary in law.

The psychological dimension is equally important. Religion, aristocracy, armies, parties, universities, corporations, and bureaucracies have all served as social machines for channeling unusually ambitious, dominant, charismatic, or status-seeking individuals. They gave such people arenas in which to compete and, at least sometimes, rules by which their ambition was constrained. If AI compresses traditional careers and weakens the role of work as a source of identity, competition for political and symbolic status may intensify. This does not imply that political leaders can be diagnosed from afar or that traits such as narcissism or Machiavellianism explain history. Political psychology is probabilistic and context-dependent [Grijalva et al., 2015; Nai & Maier, 2024]. The more defensible question is institutional: what kinds of systems reward prestige, what kinds reward domination, and how does AI lower the cost of scaling either strategy?

The book also examines a possibility that the earlier edition treated too implicitly: the Dark Enlightenment, or neoreaction. This is not simply another name for decentralization. In writings associated especially with Curtis Yarvin and Nick Land, the movement joins criticism of democratic governance to anti-egalitarian premises, concentrated sovereignty, corporate analogies for the state, and the idea that competing jurisdictions can discipline government through exit [Yarvin, 2007; Yarvin, 2008; Smith & Burrows, 2021]. Its interest for this book is analytical. It represents one possible answer to the same problem that motivates polycentric federalism: what should happen when democratic institutions appear slow while technology enables much faster management? The answers diverge sharply. A constitutional archipelago multiplies centers of public power under common rights. Neocameralism can multiply sovereign owners. Similar geometry does not imply similar politics.

Its slight preference for a polycentric, cosmolocal direction is consequently conditional. It rests on a resilience argument rather than on localist sentiment. When the future is deeply uncertain, multiple partially independent experiments can preserve options and limit correlated failure. When knowledge can travel globally while some material capacities remain local, the combination may produce both learning and continuity. Yet fragmentation becomes dangerous when externalities are global, when rich regions can secede from obligations, when local majorities can strip rights, when private owners become sovereign, or when military and AI races reward reckless jurisdictions. A resilient archipelago therefore needs a center with real capacity. The question is not how to abolish the center. It is how to prevent the center from becoming the only place where civilization can think.

THE MAP OF POSSIBLE FUTURES

A sixth future is the Dark Enlightenment or neocameralist scenario. It must be separated from the previous family because it adds explicit political premises. In Yarvin's formalism and Patchwork, diffuse democratic sovereignty is replaced by more legible ownership and executive authority; in Land's Dark Enlightenment, neoreaction is joined to a more radical accelerationist imagination. The state becomes analogous to a firm and exit becomes a principal constraint on rulers [Yarvin, 2007; Yarvin, 2008; Smith & Burrows, 2021]. The appeal is the promise of competence, responsibility, and freedom from procedural paralysis. The cost is that citizenship can become customer status and rights can become contingent on a sovereign whose power is not derived from equal political voice. AI makes the scenario more technically plausible by allowing a small executive center to administer complex societies with fewer human intermediaries.

THE AI SHOCK IS A CHANGE IN THE COST OF COGNITION

Artificial intelligence is often discussed as if its political effects followed directly from job displacement. That is too narrow. The more consequential transformation may be a decline in the cost of cognition itself. Modern organizations grew partly because coordination, accounting, legal interpretation, planning, translation, and management were expensive. Large firms and bureaucracies could afford specialist layers that small organizations could not. When agents perform a significant share of those functions, the minimum efficient scale of administration may fall. A municipality that once depended on a national ministry for expertise could obtain competent analysis locally. A small company could operate with capabilities previously available only to a multinational. A scientific community could maintain sophisticated infrastructure without a large permanent bureaucracy.

WHAT HISTORY ACTUALLY TEACHES ABOUT POLITICAL SCALE

Political scale has never moved in one direction. Human history alternates between aggregation and fragmentation because different problems reward different sizes. Small communities often have dense social knowledge and rapid accountability. Large states can mobilize armies, standardize law, pool risk, build infrastructure, and control long-distance trade. Empires solve some coordination problems by making them internal; they create others by increasing distance between rulers and local knowledge. City-states can innovate under competition; they can also spend generations at war. Any argument that one scale is naturally superior is contradicted by the diversity of successful and failed societies.

SCARCITY DOES NOT END; IT MOVES

Energy is an obvious bottleneck. Data centers convert electrical and physical infrastructure into cognitive services. The IEA's 2025 and 2026 work on energy and AI emphasizes both rising electricity demand from data centers and AI's potential to improve energy systems [IEA, 2025; IEA, 2026]. A society with abundant models but constrained grids may experience political conflicts over power allocation. Regions with cheap, reliable energy can gain bargaining power. The politics of AI may therefore resemble the politics of industrial infrastructure more than the frictionless internet imagined in earlier digital theory.

FROM THE EXTRACTIVE STATE TO THE INFRASTRUCTURE STATE

The fiscal system must also adapt. If wage income becomes a smaller share of national income, taxation tied primarily to labor becomes unstable. Consumption taxes can be regressive, while corporate profits can move across borders. Land, resource rents, capital income, and returns to public investment become more important. One plausible model is to treat part of AI infrastructure as a strategic asset held by sovereign, regional, pension, or civic funds so that citizens receive capital returns rather than relying only on redistribution after the fact.

ELITES AFTER EXPERTISE: THE IDEA OF REVOCABLE NOBILITY

Every society has elites even when it denies the word. Unequal influence can derive from wealth, military command, education, religious authority, reputation, technical skill, family networks, control of organizations, or proximity to the state. Classical elite theorists such as Pareto, Mosca, and Michels sometimes treated this persistence as nearly inevitable. More recent work is less deterministic, but the empirical observation remains: large organizations generate leadership strata and information asymmetries [Michels, 1915; Diefenbach, 2019]. The relevant question is not whether elites exist but what functions justify their privileges and how easily they can be replaced.

RELIGION, IDENTITY, STATUS, AND THE SEARCH FOR MEANING

Religion may therefore become more important even in technologically advanced societies, but not necessarily through a simple return to old institutions. AI can support personalized theology, ritual communities, historical reconstruction, translation of sacred texts, and distributed education. It can also produce synthetic prophets, charismatic avatars, automated apologetics, or systems optimized for retention and loyalty. The anthropology of costly ritual suggests why communities with demanding practices can generate trust and durability [Sosis & Bressler, 2003; Whitehouse, 2004]. AI can reduce the cost of doctrine while leaving the social value of shared sacrifice intact.

THE COMPUTATIONAL EMPIRE AND INSTITUTIONAL DARK ALIGNMENT

When functions share models and data, institutional separation can become formal rather than real. A court may be independent in law but depend on the same risk model used by prosecutors. A regulator may audit a ministry using the ministry's cloud provider and training data. A citizen may appeal an automated decision to another automated workflow optimized for the same indicators. The constitutional problem is therefore epistemic as well as legal. Separation of powers in the AI era may require separation of models, data pipelines, vendors, and evaluative methods.

DARK ENLIGHTENMENT: NEOREACTION, THE CEO-CITY, AND THE POLITICS OF EXIT

The Dark Enlightenment deserves a chapter of its own because it sits close enough to the book's fragmentation hypothesis to be confused with it and far enough away normatively that the confusion is consequential. The term commonly refers to a neoreactionary constellation associated especially with Curtis Yarvin, writing as Mencius Moldbug, and the philosopher Nick Land. Yarvin's 2007 Formalist Manifesto and his 2008 Patchwork series proposed making political power more explicit and imagining sovereign jurisdictions governed in corporate form. Land later synthesized and radicalized parts of this milieu under the label Dark Enlightenment [Yarvin, 2007; Yarvin, 2008; Smith & Burrows, 2021]. The movement is not a coherent party, and writers grouped under the label differ, but anti-democratic and anti-egalitarian themes are central enough that they cannot be treated as incidental.
