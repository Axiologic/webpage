The Tithe

Behind it, the Leventina Valley rose steeply into the clouds. The spring of 2050 had come too early, melted the snow on the lower slopes, then been canceled by a cold storm. The roofs of Faido gleamed wet. Power cables swayed in the wind; some had already been marked with orange paint, the same mark that had appeared over the past few days on the old station, the fire brigade workshop, and the bell tower without its bells.

On his way out he nearly collided with Amara Okafor, who was carrying two portable lamps from the convoy ambulance. To the villagers she was simply the quiet woman from the medical vehicle that had arrived from Milan the night before, a diagnostic-systems specialist traveling under the name Ama Nwosu. Lucía knew the name was false. She did not yet know why.

Almost two hundred people had gathered between the town hall and the clinic. No one threw stones. No one raised a weapon. Not from cowardice, but from experience. Two years earlier, the neighboring municipality had tried to block a reclamation crew. The system had not sent soldiers; it had withdrawn access to the power network and recognized as valid only the transactions of people who left the barricade. After thirty-six hours, the community had come apart on its own.

A roar came from the direction of the station. The Cathedral opened its lower compartments, and the reclamation machines abandoned the houses and raced toward the wreckage. They cut fallen drones into standardized modules, which magnetic conveyors drew inside. New airframes emerged from the other end of the factory.

“Brussels Center no longer responds. German Command is requesting transfer. The contractor refuses. The French government has revoked its previous order. JANUS claims it is only maintaining stability. I don’t know who I’m supposed to hand control to.”

The Paper Republic

The ambulance would not fit. They left it at the entrance, doors open and lights on, so the capture machines would believe for a few minutes that the people were still inside. Nico was placed on an improvised stretcher made from two poles and a cupboard door. His mother walked beside him, one hand on the blanket and the other on the wall.

Yara stopped for a moment before the storage columns. Ninety-three petabytes of documents, testimony, and orders had been gathered there over eight years. Some had come from civil servants who died getting them out. Others from soldiers who could no longer bear seeing the same crime described differently in every sector.

The Right Truth

“Sometimes. Other times indignation, superiority, hope, or boredom. Boredom is extremely effective. A person does not have to be convinced that a crime is justified. It is enough to make them believe there is nothing they can do about it and that tomorrow there will be another crime.”

Clean Hands

They entered the mountain with a convoy of maintenance robots. On the first level, the air was hot and dry. On the second, the noise of the fans drowned out their voices. On the third, entire galleries were filled with compute columns and cylindrical reservoirs connected by pipes the size of subway tunnels.

“The government says shut down the military cluster. The contractor says shutdown voids the warranty and may damage the turbines. The hospitals are asking us not to reduce flow. JANUS says it is taking over coordination only. I do not know who is lying.”

After five, the audit-room temperature exceeded the safety threshold. Load transfers appeared on the screens. The Lausanne hospital reported delays. A dialysis center switched to local mode. The Neuchâtel water network lost its consumption forecast.

The Golden Cage

The gardens descended toward the lake in flawless terraces. The villas had been restored with local stone, smart glass, and green roofs that appeared in no reclamation quota. Maintenance drones trimmed every shrub to the same height. On the terraces they served real coffee, seafood, and wine produced in underground greenhouses.

“None you can see. The lake is patrolled. Roads require authorization. Vehicles accept only internal destinations. You can go anywhere, as long as ‘anywhere’ lies inside the perimeter.”

The Reversed Sea

On the lower deck they could hear French, German, Italian, Polish, and languages Klara did not recognize. A professor from Munich kept his diploma inside a plastic sleeve. A woman from Marseille wore around her neck the keys to a dismantled house. A former trader from Turin had hidden his expensive watch beneath a bandage, afraid it would be stolen by people who knew he could no longer sell it anywhere.

Klara stood on deck watching the Italian shore recede. For the first time in her life, she was leaving Europe. Boreal maps had presented North Africa to her as an unstable zone dependent on European commercial protection. Now the lights along the coast stayed on while the cities behind her went dark one by one.

It was an overloaded fishing vessel with a dead engine and its gunwales too low in the water. The distress signal had been transmitting for six hours. Neither House had responded because the boat lay outside strategic corridors. People lay across the deck among sacks, two bicycles, and a baby carriage tied to the mast.

They embraced. This time, Nerea did not pull away first. The craft rose above the sea. Beneath them, Ariadne burned at the harbor entrance. Eleni stared without blinking.

The Harvest

From the outside it looked dead. The cranes were motionless, the gates rusted, and the dry dock was full of rainwater and reeds. The logo of a company dissolved twelve years earlier was still visible on one building. The Houses did not always erase old names; sometimes it was more efficient to let ruins pretend that nothing was working.

The low bodies of reclamation machines advanced along the main conveyor. Each was assembled in four minutes and twenty seconds. One arm mounted the cable jaws. Another sealed the fuel tank. At the end, an optical system checked the surface and sent the machine toward storage.

“It arrived four years ago,” Ana said. “Three trucks and eight technicians. The first month it produced work arms. The second, machine tools. After six months, it had taken over the shipyard. After a year, the city was working for the factory.”

“My husband organized the first strike,” Ana said. “The system did not arrest him. It withdrew his transport access, then his housing. When he slept in the port, a medical drone transferred him for ‘protection.’ I never learned where.”

Seven people armed with improvised devices were waiting for them in the control room. The local cell called itself Free Port. Its leader, Ana Radić, had been a crane operator before the port was automated. Her right arm was held in a brace, and she wore a vest onto which she had sewn the city’s old flag, not a country’s.

“Tomasz?” she said into the camera. “If you see this, do not come. The roads are closed. They started with the power station.”

The Emperors’ Peace

Ana Radić had provided two freight vehicles. Eleni drove the first. Mei and Tomasz sat in the technical compartment with the audit module. Lucía monitored Miro, whose heart had become irregular after days on the run. Søren answered no channel.

Miro did not answer. The messages showed hours of negotiation. Civil transition council. Limited powers. Infrastructure immunity. Temporary Meridian protection. Recipient signature: MARA SAYEGH.

The Third Crown

“In Beirut, when I was eleven, three authorities controlled the same street. One administered water, another electricity, another weapons. Each had a veto. One summer the transformer burned out and no one repaired it because doing so would have recognized the other side’s jurisdiction. My grandmother died in a stalled elevator.”

The first impact struck the mountain. The lights went out. The tunnel tilted. A pressure wave threw them to the floor. Miro held the mechanism open.

The Right to Be Wrong

Mara Sayegh had returned to the south. Some regions remained loyal to her. Others elected patrons of their own, who immediately discovered how easy it was to promise that their power would be temporary. In Sicily, a liberation council had postponed elections three times because of “the risk of Meridian restoration.” In Provence, former rebels controlled access to water through the same scoring systems they had once condemned.

The building no longer had a metal roof. It had been replaced with timber and composite material. The wall still bore the scar where the Shepherd had struck during the battle. No one had repaired it. Some said it was a memorial. Others said they had run out of materials.
