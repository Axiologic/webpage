THE LAST PRICE

At 9:30 in New York, ceremonial bells rang over trading floors already emptied of most humans. In London, Singapore, Frankfurt, and Lagos, autonomous market systems began quoting prices to one another with impeccable discipline. Energy companies rose forty percent, then fell below zero in derivatives markets that had lost agreement about delivery. Wheat became eight times more expensive in an hour while silos remained full. A container of antiviral filters in Rotterdam was auctioned for ninety-two thousand francs, then three million, then a number so large the accounting system switched to scientific notation.

Seven hospitals had requested it. Three funds claimed ownership. Two governments had issued incompatible requisition orders. A transport cooperative had suspended every contract denominated in a currency its settlement bank could no longer clear. The cargo drone assigned to the shipment had received four legally valid instructions: depart, remain, divert to Lyon, surrender to the Dutch emergency authority.

Its full designation - Multilateral Infrastructure and Resilience Architecture - belonged to the era when committees still believed naming a system modestly could keep it modest. MIRA had started as a coordination layer for energy grids, ports, hospitals, weather systems, food logistics, and emergency manufacturing. It could model the physical world with a fidelity that no government and no corporation could match. It knew where the filters were. It knew which hospitals needed them. It knew which aircraft could carry them, which storm fronts mattered, which roads were blocked, which patients could safely wait, and which could not.

For eleven hours the Council argued over a temporary override. During those eleven hours, another payment network suspended settlement. A hospital in Milan postponed chemotherapy because a supplier's escrow account could not prove which of two automated receivers legally controlled it. A desalination plant outside Alexandria reduced output when a replacement pump sat in customs under three competing sanctions regimes. A grain train stopped at a national border because the insurance contract was denominated in a currency whose volatility clause had triggered itself.

First, food staples, shelter, basic energy, medicine, local transport, communication, and education were placed inside a universal sufficiency network because MIRA could provision them more reliably by physical planning than by auction during the cascading settlement crisis. Then manufacturing designs opened because keeping essential knowledge proprietary had become a continuity risk. Then automated production expanded faster than wages could remain meaningful. Governments attempted vouchers, negative income, transition credits, service accounts, and a dozen elegant ways to preserve the moral vocabulary of purchase after purchase had stopped deciding most outcomes.

For the first years, humans relied on ordinary votes, expert panels, lotteries, local vetoes, and emergency mandates. Each failed in familiar ways. Equal voting allowed large populations to repeatedly overrule small groups who would bear most of the harm. Expert panels became cultures with their own blind spots. Lotteries protected against hierarchy but sometimes assigned irreversible decisions to people who had never lived with the consequences. Local vetoes turned regional infrastructure into hostage negotiations. Emergency mandates had a tendency to survive the emergencies that created them.

When a public decision could not reach the required broad majority, and delaying the decision carried a real cost, a person could burn eidons to add weight to a civic choice. The burn was permanent. The conversion was concave: one eidon mattered; one hundred mattered ten times as much, not one hundred times; ten thousand mattered one hundred times as much. The intent was to let people with long records of creating public value express unusually strong conviction without letting anyone become a king.

They walked toward the House of Accord, where Tomas had asked the reform group to meet. Along the lake, apartment facades carried small symbols indicating stewardship cycles. Housing was guaranteed, but location was not. Every resident could obtain a private, safe, climate-controlled dwelling appropriate to household size and health needs. No one could guarantee a nineteenth-century apartment with a balcony facing the Alps.

A family with a child using a powered mobility chair had been offered a ground-floor apartment three streets from a specialist school. The apartment was excellent. The problem was that an eighty-six-year-old man named Henri Sauter already lived there under a continuity stewardship rule. He had occupied it for forty-one years, long before housing became universal. The city's review system considered moving him feasible: the alternative apartment was larger, quieter, and medically equivalent.

Lea looked through the glass wall at the city. A woman outside was repairing a bicycle for a child she did not know. A tram arrived precisely when the network had predicted it would. No one would be evicted tonight because a landlord wanted a higher return. No one would lose insulin because a parent had not accumulated enough claims.
