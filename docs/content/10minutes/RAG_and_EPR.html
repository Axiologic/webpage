Abstract

The second part defines Evidence Portfolio Retrieval, or EPR, as a separate research programme motivated by the automation of scientific work. Conventional RAG usually ranks documents individually by estimated relevance to a query. EPR asks which bounded set of evidence units, considered collectively, is most useful for a downstream epistemic decision. A portfolio for novelty assessment may require a close holistic precedent, component precedents, a structural analogue, a boundary case, contradictory evidence, and an explicit account of what remains uncovered. A portfolio for peer review may require support, replication, methodological criticism, negative results, and alternative explanations. This changes the unit of optimization from an independently scored document to a budget-constrained set whose collective coverage, independence, provenance, and sufficiency can be inspected.

Chapter 1. Retrieval-Augmented Generation as a Systems Architecture

Early RAG developed alongside dense retrieval. Dense Passage Retrieval showed that a dual encoder trained on question-passage pairs could outperform a strong BM25 baseline on several open-domain question-answering datasets [2]. REALM integrated retrieval into language-model pretraining [3]. Fusion-in-Decoder encoded retrieved passages separately and fused them in the decoder, making multi-passage aggregation effective while avoiding a single quadratic encoder over the concatenated context [4]. RETRO demonstrated a different scale regime in which a language model retrieves neighbours from a database containing trillions of tokens and conditions generation on them [5]. These systems established several enduring patterns: retrieval can be trained or frozen, evidence can be consumed before or during generation, and the retrieved unit may be a passage, neighbour chunk, structured fact, image, table, or graph fragment.

This family has four different objectives that should not be collapsed. The first is knowledge access: retrieving information the model does not reliably contain. The second is grounding: constraining an answer to externally visible evidence. The third is adaptation: changing the effective knowledge base without retraining the generator. The fourth is computation: using retrieval as an intermediate operation in multi-step reasoning or agentic search. An implementation optimized for one objective may be poor at another. For example, a system that retrieves one exact policy clause can be excellent for access and grounding but inadequate for comparing conflicting policies across time. A system that recursively explores a literature graph may support synthesis but be wasteful for exact lookup.

Chapter 4. Advanced Retrieval-Augmented Architectures

Adaptive RAG changes whether and how much retrieval is used. Not every query requires external evidence, and repeated retrieval can waste latency or introduce noise. Self-RAG trains a model to retrieve, generate, and critique through special reflection decisions, allowing retrieval to be invoked selectively and outputs to be assessed for support [12]. Related approaches use uncertainty, query complexity, hallucination detection, or learned routers to choose among no retrieval, single-step retrieval, multi-step retrieval, and tool use. The architectural lesson is that retrieval policy is itself a modelled decision. The policy must be evaluated for both false negatives, where retrieval was needed but skipped, and false positives, where unnecessary retrieval harmed a correct parametric answer.

Multi-hop RAG addresses questions whose answer depends on evidence distributed across sources. HotpotQA made this requirement explicit in question answering [40], while MultiHop-RAG and later benchmarks show that standard pipelines often fail because the first query does not mention the entity needed for the second retrieval step [17]. Query decomposition generates intermediate questions. Iterative retrieval uses an answer or entity from one hop to formulate the next. Learned multi-hop retrievers attempt to model the path directly. In 2026, systems such as GRITHopper and structured program-guided retrieval continue to reduce dependence on fragile autoregressive decompositions. The core engineering problem is evidence survival: an apparently marginal passage may become decisive only after another passage establishes the bridge.

Chapter 6. Evaluation Methodology and Benchmark Design

Traditional information-retrieval metrics remain foundational. Precision@k measures the proportion of returned items judged relevant. Recall@k measures the proportion of relevant items recovered within the first k. Mean reciprocal rank rewards placing the first relevant item early. Average precision summarizes ranking quality across relevant items. Discounted cumulative gain assigns graded relevance and discounts lower ranks; normalized DCG compares the ranking with an ideal ordering. These metrics are interpretable only relative to a relevance definition and a judgement pool. In research tasks, there may be many valid evidence sets and no complete enumeration of relevant documents.

Benchmark families test different parts of the stack. BEIR evaluates zero-shot retrieval across heterogeneous domains and shows the importance of robust baselines [9]. MTEB and MMTEB evaluate embeddings across tasks and languages rather than assuming one universal representation [10]. KILT links knowledge-intensive tasks to provenance in a shared Wikipedia snapshot [41]. HotpotQA tests multi-hop question answering [40]. SciFact and FEVER evaluate claim verification with evidence [38–39]. BRIGHT tests reasoning-intensive retrieval and reveals a large gap between conventional embedding performance and queries whose relevance must be inferred [16]. MM-BRIGHT extends this challenge to multimodal retrieval [56]. No single benchmark represents scientific EPR, but together they prevent an implementation from overfitting to one easy regime.

Chapter 7. Limits of Independent Top-k Ranking in Scientific Tasks

Recent work moves toward set-level and reasoning-aware retrieval. DF-RAG diversifies factual perspectives to reduce redundant evidence and reports downstream gains rather than treating diversity as an end in itself [27]. NEST retains evidence paths that would be eliminated by locally optimal retrieval decisions, improving recall for complex reasoning [28]. COSMOS optimizes the utility of a coherent evidence subgraph under constraints [29]. User-centric evidence ranking evaluates how complementary evidence affects human verification effort [30]. RARE focuses on recall under redundant multi-hop conditions and makes visible the failure to recover all necessary facts [31]. These systems differ in task and mechanism, but collectively they support the premise that the value of evidence is conditional on what else has been retrieved.

Chapter 8. Evidence Portfolio Retrieval: Definition and Scope

Candidate generation should be deliberately plural. A lexical route preserves rare names, formulae, identifiers, and legal language. A dense route captures paraphrase and semantic similarity. Late interaction preserves local matches. Citation and reference expansion finds intellectual lineages. Metadata search enforces date, venue, source type, and status. Graph traversal follows claims, authors, methods, datasets, or patent families. Query expansion explores synonyms and historical terminology. An agentic route can issue new queries when early evidence changes the task model. EPR is intended to reuse these mature techniques and add a set-selection and evidence-accounting layer after candidate generation.

Chapter 10. Evidence Portfolio Retrieval in Scientific Automation

Novelty assessment is the most direct use case. A candidate idea can be decomposed into its purpose, mechanism, components, relations, assumptions, and claimed difference. The portfolio then seeks several distinct forms of precedent. A holistic precedent may describe nearly the same system. Component precedents show that individual elements are known. Combination precedents show that elements have already been assembled. Structural analogues reveal the same functional arrangement under different vocabulary or in another field. Boundary cases identify systems that approach the contribution but stop short of it. Independent rediscoveries show whether the idea emerged through separate lineages. Negative or contradictory evidence may show that the claimed novelty depends on a distinction without practical consequence.

Idea evaluation is a broader organizational use case. Research groups and venture studios receive many proposals that combine known components under new narratives. A portfolio can distinguish an idea with a close complete precedent from one whose components are known but whose integration is unusual. It can reveal an apparently original idea that is standard under different terminology in another field. It can also show that a technically novel mechanism addresses a problem with weak evidence of importance. Decision makers can then score opportunity, feasibility, scientific risk, and strategic relevance separately rather than treating novelty as a proxy for value.

Chapter 11. Formal Objectives and Optimization Methods

Every mathematical term should therefore correspond to an observable claim. If the objective contains role coverage, the benchmark must show that roles are identified with adequate reliability and improve downstream decisions. If it rewards independence, the system must detect source dependence accurately. If it claims lower redundancy, experts should judge the selected evidence less repetitive without losing critical replication. If it claims novelty sensitivity, it must succeed on dated, domain-covered, expert-labelled novelty cases. Equations specify hypotheses and implementation contracts; experiments determine whether they are useful.

Chapter 14. Research Agenda and Technology Development

The first research question concerns task identification. Not every research query needs a portfolio. The programme must define and detect the regimes in which top-k retrieval is structurally inadequate. Candidate indicators include multiple explicit facets, several claims, requests for comparison or assessment, novelty language, need for counterevidence, multi-hop dependencies, temporal restrictions, and requirements for independent support. A routing benchmark should include direct factual questions, synthesis questions, novelty cases, prior-art cases, review tasks, and discovery prompts. The router is evaluated on both quality and avoided cost.

Chapter 15. Conclusions: Retrieval as Evidence Selection for Scientific Decisions

EPR is therefore not an alternative search engine. It is a control layer over candidate retrieval and an output contract for reasoning-ready evidence. It decomposes a task into evidence needs, retrieves broadly through conventional channels, classifies candidates by facets and roles, models versions and dependencies, selects a bounded complementary set, and reports unresolved gaps. It can use MMR, set cover, graphs, LLMs, or learned policies. Its identity lies in the task objective and accountability structure, not in one algorithm.
