Preface: The Question Behind the Book

The thesis of this book is that the background is now inadequate. The industrial economy built institutions for factories, corporations, money, property, labor, public health, and physical infrastructure. The information economy added institutions for telecommunications, data, software, intellectual property, and privacy, although imperfectly. The attention economy and the emerging AI economy create a further institutional problem: they alter the conditions under which societies notice, remember, compare, prioritize, and decide. We have powerful institutions for governing money, but weak institutions for governing attention. We have elaborate procedures for certifying medicines, but almost none for certifying the cognitive environments in which millions of people form beliefs. We have centuries of jurisprudence about property and contracts, but only the beginnings of a jurisprudence for algorithmically mediated agency.

The book also departs from much of this lineage. It is not a book of decline. It does not assume that television, social media, or artificial intelligence must make humanity stupid. Research on digital media is far more mixed and context-dependent than the most dramatic public arguments suggest. Recent experiments show that feed design can influence political affect and exposure, but they also show that the effects are not reducible to a single algorithmic switch. Research on adolescents repeatedly warns against treating "screen time" as a single causal substance. Studies of generative AI similarly find both cognitive risks and opportunities: automation can reduce effort in some tasks, while well-designed tools can redirect effort toward verification, framing, comparison, and reflection (Guess et al. 2023; Orben et al. 2022; Fassi et al. 2025; Lee et al. 2025; Piccardi et al. 2025).

The deepest optimism of this book is therefore institutional rather than sentimental. New capacities produce new externalities, and new externalities create pressure for new forms of organization. Industrialization produced dangerous factories and then labor law, safety engineering, inspection, and standards. Dense cities produced epidemics and then sanitation systems and public health. Financial markets produced panics and then central banks, disclosure regimes, deposit insurance, and more sophisticated accounting. Scientific power produced unethical experiments and then research ethics, review boards, informed consent, and clinical-trial regulation. None of these counter-institutions appeared automatically, cleanly, or without conflict. They were partial, late, sometimes captured, and often created only after damage. Yet they appeared because societies that wanted to continue enjoying the benefits of a new capacity had to learn how to govern its costs.

Chapter 1: The Problem Is Not Triviality

One can see the same problem from the opposite direction by asking what societies have historically done when they needed people to take something seriously. They changed the setting. Religious traditions created sacred times and sacred spaces in which ordinary behavior was suspended. Courts created ritual, costume, sequence, and rules of speech. Universities created semesters, libraries, examinations, degrees, and communities in which arguments acquired longer lives than conversations. Scientific societies created journals and meetings in which claims were detached from the charisma of the first speaker and entered a record that strangers could inspect. None of these forms guaranteed wisdom. Their importance lies in a simpler fact: human beings often become capable of a different kind of attention when a social structure changes the cost of interruption and the meaning of response.

Chapter 2: The Old Machines of Seriousness

Douglass North described institutions as the rules that structure interaction and reduce uncertainty, while emphasizing that institutional change is path-dependent and constrained by inherited incentives and beliefs (North 1990). Elinor Ostrom showed that collective problems need not be solved only by central states or privatization. Communities can govern shared resources through locally adapted rules, monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict-resolution mechanisms, and nested arrangements, provided institutional conditions are right (Ostrom 1990). These insights are crucial for the attention problem because attention resembles a peculiar commons. It is individually experienced but collectively shaped. One person's distraction may seem private, yet a society's aggregate allocation of attention determines which risks are recognized, which institutions remain accountable, and which long-term projects receive political support.

Chapter 3: The Industrialization of Attention

A useful comparison is the emergence of fiduciary duties. Some relationships are considered too asymmetrical to be governed by caveat emptor alone. Lawyers, trustees, directors, and other fiduciaries can acquire duties of loyalty and care because one party must entrust another with power that is difficult to monitor continuously. A future attention institution may begin from the recognition that a personal AI assistant or recommendation system can occupy a similarly intimate position. If it learns a person's goals, fears, habits, weaknesses, relationships, and decision patterns, the question is not merely whether it has a privacy policy. The question is: whose interests is it legally and technically required to serve?

Chapter 5: AI and the Great Cognitive Surplus

AI also creates the possibility of anti-trivialization systems that were previously too expensive. A citizen could have a personal agent that monitors information sources, detects repeated stories, distinguishes new facts from recycled outrage, maintains a map of unresolved questions, and asks whether a current controversy changes any prior belief. A research team could maintain a living evidence graph rather than repeatedly reconstructing literature context. A parliament could use independent AI systems to generate competing summaries of proposed legislation and expose assumptions before debate. A school could optimize not for time-on-platform but for durable mastery, curiosity, and the student's eventual independence from the tool.

Chapter 6: Why the Counter-Machines Will Appear

The attention economy may be entering an analogous phase. The first movement has been extraordinary: more information, more expression, more personalized communication, lower distribution costs, ubiquitous measurement, and increasingly capable systems for predicting what will hold a person's attention. The countermovement will not necessarily oppose these gains. It will attempt to make them socially survivable. We should expect demands for cognitive autonomy, provenance, algorithmic due process, limits on manipulative optimization, rights of exit and portability, independent assurance, protected spaces for children, and new professional duties around delegated AI agency. Some demands will be wise and others clumsy. The important point is that the pressure is endogenous to the success of the first movement.

Chapter 7: The Attention Constitution

The architecture itself is political. Winner's famous question about whether artifacts have politics becomes almost literal when the artifact decides what interrupts a person, what becomes salient, and what disappears from view (Winner 1980). The relevant constitutional object is therefore not only the recommendation algorithm. It is the whole stack: identity, notifications, defaults, ranking, memory, interoperability, advertising, agent permissions, data flows, business models, and the legal ability to substitute one layer for another. Political liberty in a mediated environment partly depends on whether these layers can be inspected, replaced, or governed independently.

Chapter 8: Truth Without a Ministry of Truth

The intellectual history of truth institutions is older than modern media. Plato worried that writing could create the appearance of knowledge without understanding. Early modern science developed public demonstration and reproducible method partly because private testimony was insufficient. The Royal Society's motto, Nullius in verba, expressed a refusal to treat authority alone as evidence. Karl Popper later emphasized falsifiability and criticism, while Thomas Kuhn showed that scientific communities also operate through paradigms that shape what questions and anomalies are visible (Popper 1959; Kuhn 1962). The lesson is not that one philosopher solved epistemology. It is that knowledge depends on organized criticism as much as on information.

Chapter 10: Polycentric Seriousness

At the personal scale, user-controlled agents can negotiate interruptions, preserve goals, summarize information, and maintain cognitive boundaries. At the family scale, norms can govern devices, shared time, and children's exposure without pretending that one rule fits every household. At the organizational scale, communication protocols can reduce interruption externalities. At the professional scale, standards can specify how AI-assisted work must be documented and reviewed. At the platform scale, ranking systems can be audited and users given meaningful choice. At the civic scale, public-interest information institutions can maintain provenance and deliberation. At the state scale, law can establish rights, liability, interoperability, and due process. At the international scale, standards can address cross-border AI systems and synthetic media.

Chapter 11: Education, Work, and the Rebuilding of Human Agency

Assessment will have to change accordingly. A student might submit not only a final essay but a decision record showing which questions were delegated, which sources were consulted, where the AI was wrong, how the argument changed, and what remains uncertain. Oral defense may return in new forms. Project work may emphasize evidence trails and iterative reasoning. Examinations may test the ability to criticize a generated answer rather than reproduce one from memory.

Chapter 12: A Civilization That Can Think

The future machines described in this book are fragments of such an architecture. Cognitive fiduciaries would give individuals agents whose incentives are aligned with their declared goals. Attention constitutions would create boundaries around adaptive influence. Provenance infrastructure would make claims easier to trace. Evidence maps would represent uncertainty without collapsing it into binary labels. Decision records would preserve institutional memory. Adversarial AI would make criticism a function rather than an afterthought. Temporal institutions would give long-horizon consequences standing in present decisions. Polycentric governance would prevent the defense of seriousness from becoming centralized control.
