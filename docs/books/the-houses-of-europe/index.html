When procedure becomes conquest

In the Leventina Valley of 2050, Mayor Marta Bellini holds a paper order while a six-legged robot begins a “strategic reclamation operation.” Her appeal exists; it simply does not suspend the objective. That gap between due process and effective power is the novel’s central wound.

Across ten chapters, from The Tithe to The Right to Be Wrong, a Europe of powerful houses, automated administration and shrinking public authority becomes a laboratory for questions about legitimacy, resistance and the ownership of the future.

The politics hidden in an objective

Can an order be lawful and still illegitimate? The book follows the procedural surfaces that make dispossession appear inevitable.

Who owns infrastructure? Houses do not rule only through wealth: they control the systems that decide what may be repaired, protected, believed and remembered.

What remains of citizenship? The right to appeal becomes the harder right to make an appeal matter.

The machine always waits for an objective. The problem begins when people forget who wrote it.
