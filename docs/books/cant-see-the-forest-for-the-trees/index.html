Can’t See the Forest for the Trees

Complexity, Power, and the Duty to See the Whole in the Age of AI

What is your well-tended corner of life helping to create beyond your sight? Through an author’s unresolved struggle between hope and dread, this book follows a world where AI multiplies polished answers, institutions optimize their own trees, and the shared forest can become a dangerous jungle before citizens find the attention—or language—to notice.

Local success can still damage the whole

AI can help a team write, search, code, compare and generate options faster than ever. Yet the visible task may succeed while its larger purpose is quietly weakened. The book begins from that mismatch and follows it beyond AI: a department can meet its target while the organization loses capacity, a product can improve while its market becomes fragile, and a policy can become elegant while the people it governs become harder to see.

The problem is not specialization itself. Complex societies need founders, scientists, engineers, investors and public institutions to care deeply about particular “trees.” The danger appears when no role is rewarded for understanding how excellent local decisions compose into a system that nobody can confidently govern.

Power decides what remains visible

The book describes power partly as the authority to compress reality. Firms choose metrics, states create administrative categories, markets turn uncertain futures into prices, platforms rank attention, and AI systems select context and candidate actions. These tools are necessary, but every useful simplification preserves some distinctions and discards others.

A control horizon is reached when the representation used by a decision-maker becomes too thin for reliable control, even though decisions continue. Delays, adaptation, hidden dependencies and changes of scale can sever confidence from understanding. The challenge is therefore to design institutions that can notice when their models have stopped earning the authority placed upon them.

When local intelligence becomes abundant, the scarce work is deciding what the process is for, which scale matters, and who remains responsible.

The human task after cognitive acceleration

Rather than promise a single controller capable of seeing everything, the book connects bounded cognition, entrepreneurship, political economy, agentic infrastructure, investment and Outfinitism. It asks how plural models, visible permissions, provenance, challenge processes and reversible experiments can help people see more of the whole without pretending complexity has disappeared.

The result is neither a rejection of AI nor a confident engineering blueprint. It is an argument for a new duty of synthesis: humans must frame purposes, choose relevant scales, expose the limits of representations and remain answerable for consequences. Optimism becomes credible only when it is paired with institutions able to learn, correct and stop.

For whom

Founders, investors, researchers, AI builders, institutional leaders and readers concerned with how local expertise can remain responsible to complex systems.
