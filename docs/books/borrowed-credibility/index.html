Borrowed Credibility

How Science Decides What to Believe

Follow the path from necessary trust to evidence networks, and examine how scientific institutions can make their shortcuts more visible and corrigible.

Credibility is infrastructure

Trust is not an embarrassment in scientific life. A researcher has to choose which papers, collaborators, methods, and institutions deserve scarce time. A pedigree, however, can rationally determine where inspection starts without determining what the inspection should conclude.

The distinction is the book’s central guardrail: reputation may guide attention; evidence should govern belief. Once the two are fused, accumulated visibility becomes self-validating and untested work can inherit authority it has not earned.

How advantage compounds

Publication, funding, career incentives, gatekeeping, and professional networks shape which questions are asked and which researchers can remain in the field. The book follows both the practical benefits of those filters and the knowledge they can leave unproduced when access to credibility is uneven.

A proxy can help us choose what to inspect. It cannot settle what is true.

From reputation networks to evidence networks

Lower-cost AI-assisted evaluation expands what institutions could check: provenance, methods, data, replications, conflicts, uncertainty, and counterarguments. The proposal is not automated judgment by score, but an auditable research culture in which authority makes itself increasingly answerable to inspectable work.

For whom

Researchers, editors, funders, policymakers, and readers concerned with scientific credibility and institutional reform.

Editorial record

Research edition, 2026. It treats credibility as a necessary coordination tool, not an argument for cynicism about science.
